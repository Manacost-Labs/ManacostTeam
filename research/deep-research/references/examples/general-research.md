# General Research Example

This is a process example, not a factual answer. It avoids fabricated studies or statistics.

## Request

```text
research: exhaustive, statistics-heavy, fact-check
Does a four-day workweek improve productivity without harming employee wellbeing?
```

## Interpretation

The wording combines at least two outcomes and implies causality. Define:

- four-day week: reduced hours or compressed hours;
- productivity: output per worker, output per hour, revenue, service level, or self-report;
- wellbeing: burnout, stress, satisfaction, absence, or retention;
- population: industry, employer size, geography, and worker type;
- time horizon and comparison condition.

## Research tree

```text
Four-day workweek outcomes
├── Intervention definition
│   ├── reduced hours with equal pay
│   └── compressed schedule
├── Productivity
│   ├── objective operational metrics
│   ├── self-reported productivity
│   └── persistence after pilot
├── Wellbeing
│   ├── burnout and stress
│   ├── job satisfaction and retention
│   └── work intensification harms
├── Evidence quality
│   ├── randomized or controlled evidence
│   ├── selection into pilots
│   ├── attrition and reporting bias
│   └── funder/advocacy conflicts
└── Heterogeneity and contradictions
    ├── industry and role
    ├── geography and regulation
    ├── short versus long horizon
    └── organizations that abandoned the model
```

## Source families

- Primary trial reports, study protocols, datasets, and employer operational records.
- Peer-reviewed research and systematic reviews with design inspection.
- Government or labor-statistics context.
- Expert methodological critiques.
- Employee and employer community evidence for failure modes, explicitly nonrepresentative.
- Contradiction searches for null results, work intensification, selection bias, and abandoned trials.

## Validation emphasis

- Do not combine reduced-hours and compressed-hours interventions.
- Do not treat pilot participation as random.
- Separate objective output from satisfaction surveys.
- Preserve data window and post-pilot follow-up.
- Do not turn association or before/after change into a universal causal claim.
- Report heterogeneous and unresolved results rather than one average slogan.

## Completion

Each outcome and major population branch needs independent saturation. The final answer may conclude that the evidence differs by intervention and context; “mixed” is not a shortcut for skipping conflict analysis.
